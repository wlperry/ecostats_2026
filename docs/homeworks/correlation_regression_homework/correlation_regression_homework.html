Homework 5 — Correlation & Regression

The transformation you have to find

One hundred stream invertebrate species, body mass against population density: the same regression workflow from class, on data where the first answer you get is the wrong one.
Author

Bill Perry

Worksheet: The Transformation You Have to Find

📥 Stream invertebrate data 📥 Tools script (.R) 📥 Script skeleton (.R)

The study. Small animals are common and big animals are rare — that is one of the oldest patterns in ecology. This dataset holds 100 stream invertebrate species: for each one, the average body mass of an individual and the population density at which that species lives.

The question. Does body mass predict population density, and if so, how steeply?

How this relates to class. You are running Parts 5–8 of the in-class worksheet again: fit, interpret, partition into sums of squares, check the residuals. Same order, same functions. The difference is that in class the diagnostics confirmed a model that was already fine. Here they will tell you to throw your first model away, and you have to work out what to do instead.

Columns: body_mass_mg (average individual body mass, in milligrams) and density_per_m2 (individuals per square metre).

Note

Before you start

You are not reproducing anyone’s published analysis, and there is no hidden “right” p-value to hit. You are graded on whether your conclusion follows from your diagnostics.

Part 6 · The slope is the point

On a log–log plot, the slope is an exponent: density ∝ mass^slope.

Ecological theory predicts something specific here. If every species in a stream uses roughly the same total amount of energy per square metre, and an individual’s energy use scales as mass^0.75, then density should scale as mass^−0.75. That is the energetic equivalence prediction.

So the interesting hypothesis is not “is the slope zero?” It is “is the slope −0.75?”
