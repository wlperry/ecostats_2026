Activity: Study Design & Sampling

Design and sampling

In-class activity: reading a design off a real randomized, blinded, unbalanced experiment — Wright & Czeisler’s circadian light study — and testing whether the placebo arm was needed.
Author

Bill Perry

Worksheet: Reading a Design

📥 Knees data 📥 Tools script (.R) 📥 Script skeleton (.R)

The study. Wright & Czeisler (2002) asked whether bright light shone on the back of the knee could reset a person’s circadian clock, the way light in the eyes does. Each volunteer was randomly assigned to one of three treatments and their circadian rhythm shift was measured in hours (negative = the clock shifted later).

  • control — no light at all
  • knee — light on the back of the knee
  • eyes — light in the eyes

Why this study is here. knee is not really a third treatment. It is a placebo — it puts a person through the entire procedure (the apparatus, the room, the night, the measurement) while delivering light somewhere it should not work. Today you decide whether that arm earned its place.

This is Whitlock & Schluter, Example 15.1.

Part 3 · Did the placebo do anything?

This is the bias question: does going through the procedure, without useful light, shift the clock on its own?

▶ Run this:

# ── Part 3.1 · Did the placebo do anything? ─────────────────────────────
knees_df %>%
  filter(treatment %in% c("control", "knee")) %>%
  t.test(shift_h ~ treatment, data = .)

Part 4 · Did the real treatment do anything?

▶ Run this:

# ── Part 4.1 · Did the real treatment do anything? ──────────────────────
knees_df %>%
  filter(treatment %in% c("control", "eyes")) %>%
  t.test(shift_h ~ treatment, data = .)

▶ Run this — how big is it?

# ── Part 4.2 · Did the real treatment do anything? ──────────────────────
knees_df %>%
  filter(treatment %in% c("control", "eyes")) %>%
  cohens_d(shift_h ~ treatment, data = .)

✏️ Your turn: p = __________ , d = __________ .

Note

Two comparisons, both decided in advance

We ran two tests against the same control rather than one test across all three groups. That is legitimate here because we chose both comparisons before looking — each one answers a specific design question. Three groups at once is a one-way ANOVA, which is Week 8.

Part 6 · The figure you hand in

This is the only plot that gets labels, a theme and a title — because this is the one being graded on presentation.

▶ Run this:

# ── Part 6.1 · The figure you hand in ───────────────────────────────────
knees_summary <- knees_df %>%
  group_by(treatment) %>%
  reframe(summary_stats(shift_h))

final_plot <- ggplot(knees_summary, aes(treatment, mean)) +
  geom_col(fill = "grey70", width = 0.6) +
  geom_errorbar(aes(ymin = mean - se, ymax = mean + se), width = 0.15) +
  labs(
    title = "Circadian shift by light treatment",
    subtitle = "Mean ± 1 SE; light on the knee behaves like no light at all",
    x = "Light treatment",
    y = "Circadian shift (hours)"
  ) +
  theme_regular(base_size = 12)

final_plot

ggsave(
  "figures/knees_figure.pdf",
  plot   = final_plot,
  width  = 6,
  height = 4,
  units  = "in"
)
