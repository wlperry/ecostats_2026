Today’s Overview

One idea organizes the whole lecture:

A design has exactly two jobs — get rid of bias, and shrink sampling error. Every technique today does one or the other.

  • Bias → controls, randomization, blinding
  • Sampling error → replication, balance, blocking
  • Then: sampling layouts in the field, and which analysis each design leads to

Part 1 · Bias and Sampling Error

The Two Ways an Estimate Goes Wrong

Your study produces an estimate. It can miss the truth two different ways:

  • Bias — your estimate is centred on the wrong value. Collect more data and you converge, confidently, on the wrong answer.
  • Sampling error — your estimate is centred on the right value but bounces around it. Collect more data and it tightens up.

This distinction is the whole lecture, because the two problems have completely different cures:

Problem Gets worse with Cured by
Bias nothing — more data does not help controls, randomization, blinding
Sampling error small n, high variance replication, balance, blocking
Important

⚠️ Why this matters more than any test

A p-value describes sampling error only. No statistical test can detect bias in your own design. That is why design comes first.

A confounder is a bias machine: it moves the estimate off the truth, and it does not care how many samples you take.

Start From the Question

Before either job, one question: what is your research question?

Gotelli & Ellison’s four canonical forms:

  • Spatial or temporal pattern in Y?
  • Effect of factor X on Y?
  • Are values of Y consistent with hypothesis H?
  • What is the best estimate of parameter θ?

Each one implies a different design. “I will go measure some things and see what turns up” implies none of them.

📖 Reference

Gotelli & Ellison, Ch. 6 — Designing Successful Field Studies.

Part 2 · Causality and the Inference Gradient

How Much Causal Weight Can a Design Carry?

  • The question we usually want answered is what causes Y?
  • Statistics has no test for causality — the design carries the causal claim, not the analysis
  • Strength of causal inference runs on a gradient, and what moves you up it is control of confounding

From weakest to strongest:

  1. Observational field study
  2. Field study with measured covariates
  3. Randomized, controlled experiment
Warning

⚠️ The same regression, three different claims

lm(y ~ x) returns the identical output at all three levels. What changes is what you are entitled to say about it.

Causality Example — Spiders and Lizards

Hypothesis: on small islands, lizard predation controls spider density.

Ask, in order:

  • What type of study could test this?
  • What could produce a lizard–spider correlation with no causal link at all?
  • What would you have to measure to rule that out? What would you have to manipulate?
Note

✅ Where we are going

We will answer this twice today — once as a natural experiment, once as a manipulative one — and the design, not the statistics, is what differs.

Part 3 · Natural and Manipulative Experiments

Natural Experiments

Not experiments at all — you use natural variation in the predictor. Survey plots across an existing gradient of lizard density.

What you gain: real scale, real systems, long time spans, questions you could never manipulate.

What you give up:

  • Direction of causation is not identified — lizards may eat spiders, or spiders may feed lizards
  • Uncontrolled variables ride along with the predictor
Note

📖 Reference

Whitlock & Schluter §14.7 — “What if you can’t do experiments?” — is exactly this problem, and covers matching and adjusting for covariates as the two partial fixes.

Strengthening a Natural Experiment

You cannot randomize, so you attack confounding directly:

  • Reduce it — choose plots that are alike in everything except lizard density (this is matching)
  • Adjust for it — measure the covariates and put them in the model
  • Name it — you cannot adjust for a confounder you never wrote down

Know which one you have:

  • A confounder drives both X and Y — you must adjust for it
  • A mediator sits on the causal path X → M → Y — adjusting for it removes the effect you are trying to measure
Warning

⚠️ Same variable, opposite advice

Moisture as a confounder: control for it. Moisture as a mediator: controlling for it destroys your result. Only the biology tells you which — no diagnostic will.

Manipulative Experiments

You set the predictor yourself and measure the response. Randomized and controlled, this is the strongest design available.

What it costs you — Gotelli & Ellison’s trade-offs:

  • Scale for replication — replicable plots are small plots
  • Organism for tractability — small, short-lived species
  • Treatments for replication — every extra treatment level costs replicates
  • Confounding does not disappear; you now own it

Cedar Creek Ecosystem Science Reserve

Press, Pulse, Snapshot, Trajectory

Gotelli & Ellison’s two axes for manipulations — and they answer different questions:

  • Pulse — apply the treatment once, then walk away. Measures recovery: how fast does the system bounce back?
  • Press — maintain the treatment continuously. Measures the new equilibrium the system settles into.

And for sampling the response:

  • Snapshot — measure once at the end. Cheap, and blind to everything in between.
  • Trajectory — measure repeatedly through time. Expensive, and the only way to see dynamics.
Important

⚠️ A snapshot can hide a complete reversal

Many treatment effects change sign as a system responds. Measure once, at one time, and you get whichever answer that moment happened to hold.

Mesocosms let you press a treatment and follow a trajectory — at the cost of scale.

Part 4 · Job One: Getting Rid of Bias

Controls — and Why One Is Usually Not Enough

A control is a unit treated exactly like the manipulated units, except for the one thing under investigation.

That word exactly is where designs fail. Your manipulation almost always does something besides the thing you care about:

  • Toxicology: controls get injected too — same needle, same handling, no toxin
  • Predator exclosures: a cage changes flow, light and sediment. You need two controls — an open plot and a cage-with-holes
  • Anything you attach, bury, or visit does something merely by being attached, buried or visited
Note

Vocabulary

The second kind is a procedural or sham control. Next slide: real data where forgetting it would have produced a completely wrong paper.

A Sham Control Earns Its Keep — Sponges on Mangrove Roots

Ellison, Farnsworth & Twilley (1996) asked whether epibiont sponges promote mangrove root growth. Four treatments, 14 roots each:

Important

⚠️ The paper you would have written without the foam

  • Control vs Foam: p = 0.021 — the inert object significantly increases root growth
  • Foam vs sponges: p = 0.33 — the living sponges add nothing detectable

With only a bare control you would have reported “sponges promote root growth,” with a real p-value and a clean figure. The effect is mostly having an object attached.

The sham control is what stands between a correct result and a confident wrong one.

Randomization

Randomization is how you deconfound the variables you never thought of. It works at two levels, and both matter:

  1. Selection — which units enter the study. Makes the sample represent the population.
  2. Assignment — which unit gets which treatment. Makes the groups exchangeable before treatment.

Haphazard is not random. “Whichever tree I reached first” correlates with trail access, which correlates with light, soil and disturbance.

# Selection: 10 trees from the 100 in the stand
selected <- sample(1:100, 10)

# Assignment: 5 to treatment, the rest to control
treated <- sample(selected, 5)

tibble(tree = selected,
       group = if_else(tree %in% treated, "treatment", "control")) %>%
  arrange(group)
# A tibble: 10 × 2
    tree group    
   <int> <chr>

 7    25 treatment
 8    74 treatment

10    24 treatment
Tip

🖐 set.seed() makes it reproducible, not less random

Record the seed and anyone can reconstruct exactly which trees you assigned where.

Blinding

The measurer’s expectations leak into the measurements. This is a bias, so no amount of data fixes it.

  • Single-blind — the subject does not know the treatment
  • Double-blind — the person measuring does not know either
  • A placebo is what makes subject-blinding possible at all

W&S spend most of Ch. 14’s opening on this because medicine learned it the hard way. Ecology mostly has not.

Important

⚠️ This lands on our own study

You measured needles knowing which side of the tree they came from, and knowing the hypothesis. Nobody cheated. But “is this needle bent or straight, include it or not” is a judgement call, and judgement calls drift toward the expected answer.

The fix is cheap: someone relabels the envelopes, you measure blind to side.

Expectation is a powerful thing.

Part 5 · Job Two: Shrinking Sampling Error

Replication — and at What Scale

Replication does two jobs: it shrinks the standard error, and it is the only way to estimate variability at all.

The hard part is never how many. It is of what.

  • Fire and soil invertebrates: samples from burnt and unburnt parts of one forest. What population does that describe? One forest.
  • Copper and barnacles: 2 aquaria, 5 plates each. n = 2, not 10.
  • Sewage and water quality: 10 samples above the outfall, 10 below. n = 1 above and n = 1 below — one location each.
Warning

⚠️ The rule

Replicates must vary at the scale your hypothesis applies to. Anything finer is pseudoreplication (Hurlbert 1984), and it shrinks your SE without adding information — a bias dressed as precision.

We Already Fixed This Once

Our own pine data has 64 rows and 8 trees’ worth of sides — 4 trees, two sides each.

Balance

For a fixed total effort, equal group sizes give the smallest standard error on the difference.

  • 10 and 10 beats 15 and 5, using the same 20 units
  • Balance also makes the tests robust: unequal variances hurt far less when n is equal
  • Unbalanced designs are harder to interpret in ANOVA — sums of squares stop being unique (we will hit this in Week 9)
Tip

🖐 When imbalance is not your fault

Mortality, lost plots and weather happen. Balance is a design goal, not a moral requirement — but plan for balance, and report what you actually ended up with.

Note

📖 Reference

Whitlock & Schluter §14.5 — reducing the influence of sampling error: replication, balance, blocking, and choosing extreme treatment levels.

Note

Extreme treatments

If you want to detect a dose response with few replicates, spread your doses wide. Four doses clustered near the middle waste your replicates on a range where nothing happens.

Blocking — and What Our Paired Design Really Was

Blocking: group the units into sets that are alike, then randomize treatments within each set. Variation among blocks is removed from the comparison instead of contaminating it.

  • Chambers differ? Run every temperature in every chamber — block by chamber
  • Days differ? Run all treatments each day — block by time
  • Ridiculously variable sites? Put all treatments at each site
Important

✅ A paired design is a block of size two

Two sides of the same tree share genotype, soil, age and history. Pairing removes all of it from the comparison. That is why 7 trees do the work of 88.

Non-independence you ignore costs you. Non-independence you plan for pays you. Blocking is how you get paid.

Blocked designs need blocked analyses — Week 9 and Week 10.

Every tree slopes the same way. The trees sit at different heights. Blocking keeps the slope and throws away the heights.

Independence and Autocorrelation

Most tests assume observations are independent — knowing one tells you nothing about another.

Autocorrelation is the standard violation: things near each other in space or time resemble each other.

“Everything is related to everything, but near things are more related than distant things.”

  • Samples 1 m apart on a transect are not two independent samples
  • Measurements on consecutive days are not independent
  • Offspring of one mother are not independent of each other

The fix is either design (space samples beyond the correlation range) or model (Week 10 and beyond).

Part 6 · Sampling Layouts in the Field

Simple Random and Stratified

Simple random — every unit has an equal chance of selection. Number the units, draw with a random number generator.

  • What nearly every test assumes you did
  • Genuinely awkward in the field; haphazard is the common substitute, and it is not the same thing

Stratified — if the population has distinct strata, sample each one separately, with n proportional to stratum size.

  • More precise than simple random when strata differ, because within-stratum variance is all that is left
  • Strata must go into the model, not be ignored

Simple random

Stratified

Cluster and Systematic

Cluster — sample subunits nested inside larger units. Used when travel or access makes anything else impossible.

  • Cheap, and less precise than simple random for the same total n
  • The clusters are your replicates → nested ANOVA (Week 9) or a mixed model (Week 10)

Systematic — units evenly spaced; the transect is the ecological classic.

  • Ideal for describing change along a gradient
  • One real risk: your spacing lines up with a periodicity in the system

BACI — When You Cannot Randomize an Impact

Nobody randomizes an oil spill. Before–After–Control–Impact is the design for impacts you did not choose:

  • Measure before and after, at both an impact site and a control site
  • The evidence is the interaction: did impact-versus-control change from before to after?
  • Before/after alone confounds the impact with time. Control/impact alone confounds it with place. You need both.
Warning

⚠️ Look at the figure — what is missing?

Visited beach versus restricted beach, measured once. Real difference, real data. But the beaches may always have differed. With no “before,” the impact and the site are inseparable.

BACI is a factorial design with an interaction → Week 9.

Part 7 · Sample Size Is a Design Decision

How Big, Decided Before You Start

We did the mechanics last lecture. The design point is the one worth keeping:

  • Power rises with the effect size, with √n, and with α — and falls as σ rises
  • Type II error is β. Power is 1 − β.
  • Two ways to run it a priori: solve for the n you need, or for the effect size your affordable n could detect
# Our pine pilot, unpaired vs paired — same trees, same needles
pwr.t.test(d = d_paired, power = 0.80,
           sig.level = 0.05, type = "paired")$n
[1] 6.406838

44 trees per side (88 total) unpaired, versus 7 trees paired. Nothing changed but the design.

Note

Plan for precision, not just for power

W&S §14.6 frames this as planning the width of your confidence interval. “How precise will my estimate be?” is often the more honest question than “will I get a star?”

Important

⚠️ Retrospective power, one more time

Computing power from the effect size you observed re-expresses your p-value and tells you nothing new. Use it to plan the next study. To argue for no effect, use an equivalence test or a CI that excludes anything meaningful.

Part 8 · From Design to Analysis

Your Design Chooses Your Analysis

This is Gotelli & Ellison Ch. 7’s whole point, and it is also the rest of this course. Write the design down and the analysis is already decided:

Your design The analysis When
One continuous predictor Regression Week 6
Several continuous predictors Multiple regression Week 7
One categorical factor One-way ANOVA Week 8
Two crossed factors, or BACI Two-way ANOVA + interaction Week 9
Subsamples inside units; clusters Nested ANOVA Week 9
Blocks, repeated measures, split-plot Mixed model Week 10
Counts, or presence/absence GLM Week 11
Factor plus a continuous covariate ANCOVA Week 12
Important

You cannot choose your analysis at the end

You chose it the day you decided what to measure and how to lay it out. Everything after that is bookkeeping.

Summary

  1. A design has two jobs: kill bias, shrink sampling error. No test does either for you.
  2. Bias → controls (usually more than one), randomization at both levels, blinding.
  3. Sampling error → replication at the right scale, balance, blocking.
  4. Natural experiments buy realism with causal ambiguity; manipulations buy inference with scale. Press or pulse, snapshot or trajectory — choose deliberately.
  5. Replicate the thing your hypothesis is about. Pseudoreplication buys false precision.
  6. Your design has already chosen your analysis.
Important

The sponge slide, if you remember nothing else

The inert foam grew roots almost as well as the living sponges. Without that sham control, the paper reports a sponge effect — correctly analysed, properly significant, and wrong.

Design is not the paperwork before the science. It is the science.
