The Study · Does a Big Claw Act as a Radiator?

A male fiddler crab has one hugely enlarged claw. Darnell & Munguia asked whether it doubles as a heat radiator, dumping heat so the crab can stay out foraging in the sun.

Four groups of 21 crabs each, body temperature above ambient measured in the field:

  • intact male — the control
  • male major removed — the big claw taken off
  • male minor removed — a small claw taken off, the procedural control
  • female — never had a major claw at all

If the big claw sheds heat, removing it should make males hotter.

crab_df <- read_csv("data/fiddler_crabs.csv") %>%
  mutate(crab_type = factor(crab_type))

A balanced design

Equal n in every group — which makes the degrees-of-freedom arithmetic below clean, and the test robust.

Part 2 · ANOVA Is Regression

Same Machinery, Different Predictor

Regression

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_i + \varepsilon_i\]

  • X is continuous
  • H₀: β₁ = 0
  • One slope

ANOVA

\[Y_{ij} = \mu + A_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\]

  • \(A_i\) is the effect of group i
  • H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μₖ
  • One coefficient per group, minus one

Both:

  • Partition total variation into explained and left over
  • Divide each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom
  • Test with an F-ratio of the two mean squares
Important

✅ The same lm() call

# regression
lm(absorbance ~ nitrate, data = df)

# ANOVA
lm(body_temp_c ~ crab_type, data = df)

The only difference is that crab_type is a factor, so R builds dummy variables for you — exactly as it did last week.

🗳️ DECIDE · Why Not Just Run All the t-tests?

With four crab groups there are six possible pairwise t-tests. Suppose every group really has the same mean, and you run all six at α = 0.05.

What is the chance you get at least one “significant” result?

(a) 5% — that is what α = 0.05 means

(b) About 12%

(c) About 26%

(d) 30% — 6 × 5%

Warning

✍️ Write your letter down, then vote.

Note

The question to ask

What is the chance of getting no false positives in six independent tries?

The Multiple-Comparisons Problem

Important

✅ The answer is (c) — about 26%.

\[1 - (1 - 0.05)^6 = 0.265\]

Each test has a 95% chance of behaving. Six in a row behave only 73.5% of the time, so you have roughly a one in four chance of at least one false positive — before you have learned anything.

(d) is the common guess. Adding probabilities overshoots, because the ways of being wrong overlap.

ANOVA’s job is to ask one question first: is there any difference at all among these groups?

One test, one α, one p-value. Only if it rejects do you go looking for which groups differ — and then you use a post-hoc method that controls the error rate across that whole family of comparisons.

With 10 groups there are 45 pairs, and the chance of at least one false positive is over 90%.

Part 3 · The Logic

ANOVA’s Question

Even if every group had the same true mean, the sample means would differ, just from sampling error.

ANOVA asks whether the sample means differ more than sampling error alone would produce:

  • \(MS_{among}\) — how spread out the group means are
  • \(MS_{within}\) — how spread out individuals are inside their own group

\[F = \frac{MS_{among}}{MS_{within}}\]

  • H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = μ₄
  • H₁: at least one mean differs

Where the Variation Goes

Every point’s distance from the grand mean splits into two pieces, exactly as in regression:

\[\underbrace{(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y})}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{(\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})}_{\text{among}} + \underbrace{(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i)}_{\text{within}}\]

Square and sum over every observation:

\[SS_{total} = SS_{among} + SS_{within}\]

The dashed line is the grand mean; the coloured bars are the group means.

Part 4 · Partitioning the Sum of Squares

The Sums of Squares

\[SS_{total} = \sum_{i}\sum_{j}(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y})^2\]

\[SS_{among} = \sum_{i} n_i(\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2\]

\[SS_{within} = \sum_{i}\sum_{j}(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i)^2\]

with a groups and \(n_i\) observations in group i:

  • \(df_{among} = a - 1\) = 3
  • \(df_{within} = N - a\) = 80
  • \(df_{total} = N - 1\) = 83
Tip

🖐 Note the \(n_i\)

Each group mean’s deviation is weighted by that group’s own sample size. With a balanced design every \(n_i\) is the same and it simplifies to \(n\sum(\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2\) — but only then.

Our design is balanced at n = 21, so both forms agree here.

Computing Them by Hand

grand_mean <- mean(crab_df$body_temp_c)

crab_parts <- crab_df %>%
  group_by(crab_type) %>%
  mutate(group_mean = mean(body_temp_c)) %>%
  ungroup()

ss_total  <- sum((crab_parts$body_temp_c - grand_mean)^2)
ss_among  <- sum((crab_parts$group_mean  - grand_mean)^2)
ss_within <- sum((crab_parts$body_temp_c - crab_parts$group_mean)^2)

c(total = ss_total, among = ss_among, within = ss_within)
   total    among   within 
6.108929 2.641310 3.467619 
ss_among + ss_within
[1] 6.108929

2.641 + 3.468 = 6.109 — exactly, as it must.

Important

✅ The same identity as regression

  • \(SS_{among}\) here is \(SS_{regression}\) there
  • \(SS_{within}\) here is \(SS_{residual}\) there

Different names, same partition, same arithmetic.

(a) All four crab types have different mean temperatures

(b) At least one crab type differs from at least one other

(c) Removing the major claw raises body temperature

Note

The question to ask

What was the null hypothesis, exactly?

A test can only tell you about the hypothesis it tested.

What F Does and Does Not Say

Important

✅ The answer is (b).

H₀ was “all four means are equal.” Rejecting it says at least one differs — not which, not how many, and not in which direction.

(a) claims far more than the test supports. (c) is a directional claim about one specific pair; that needs a post-hoc comparison, and Part 8 shows it is not even true. (d) misreads R²: 0.432 is the variance explained by crab type, which bundles sex and two surgeries together, not by the claw alone.

Warning

⚠️ The most common ANOVA mistake

Reporting a significant F and then describing whichever group means look farthest apart, as though the F test had said so.

It did not. The F test is an entry ticket, not an answer.

Part 5 · The F-Ratio

What F Actually Is

\[F = \frac{MS_{among}}{MS_{within}}\]

  • \(MS_{within}\) estimates the background variance σ² — pooled from inside the groups, where the treatment cannot reach
  • \(MS_{among}\) estimates that same σ², plus anything real that separates the group means
  • If H₀ is true, both estimate σ² and F ≈ 1

Here F = 20.31 — the group means are 20 times further apart than within-group noise alone can explain.

Tip

🖐 Why F is never negative

It is a ratio of two variances, and a variance is a sum of squares. The direction of a difference disappears the moment you square it — which is why a significant F cannot tell you which way anything went.

F and t Are the Same Test

Take just two of the four groups — intact males and females — and test them both ways:

crab_two <- crab_df %>%
  filter(crab_type %in% c("intact male", "female")) %>%
  droplevels()

t.test(body_temp_c ~ crab_type,
       data = crab_two, var.equal = TRUE)$statistic
       t 
5.958321 
anova(lm(body_temp_c ~ crab_type,
         data = crab_two))[1, "F value"]
[1] 35.50158

t = 5.958, so t² = 35.5 — and F = 35.5.

Identical, with identical p-values.

Important

✅ ANOVA generalises the t-test

With two groups they are the same test. With three or more, the t-test has nowhere to go and ANOVA does.

This is the same t² = F identity you proved on a regression slope in Week 6.

Variance Explained

\[R^2 = \frac{SS_{among}}{SS_{total}} = \frac{2.64}{6.11} = 0.432\]

Crab type accounts for about 43.2% of the variation in body temperature. The rest is individual crabs being individual crabs.

summary(crab_model)$r.squared
[1] 0.4323687
Note

Significant and partial, at the same time

p = 7.00e-10 says the group differences are not noise. R² = 0.43 says they explain under half of what is going on.

Both are true, and a results section should carry both.

Part 6 · Assumptions and Diagnostics

The Three Assumptions

One-way ANOVA assumes:

  1. Independence — observations do not depend on each other. This one comes from the design, and no plot can rescue it
  2. Normality — of the residuals, not of the raw response
  3. Equal variance (homoscedasticity) across groups

ANOVA is fairly robust to mild departures from 2 and 3 when the design is balanced, which is one more reason to plan for equal n.

Warning

⚠️ Normality of what?

Not body_temp_c. Not each group separately. The residuals of the fitted model — exactly as in regression.

A response that looks bimodal because the groups differ is perfectly fine; that is the effect you are testing.

The Four Diagnostic Plots

The same four panels you read for a regression:

  1. Residuals vs fitted — with a factor, the points stack in vertical columns, one per group. You are looking for equal spread in each column
  2. QQ plot — residuals against normal
  3. Scale-location — same check, easier to see
  4. Residuals vs leverage — influential points

Levene’s Test — Equal Variances

leveneTest(body_temp_c ~ crab_type, data = crab_df)
Levene's Test for Homogeneity of Variance (center = median)
      Df F value Pr(>F)
group  3  0.1587 0.9238
      80

H₀ is “the variances are equal”, so a large p-value is the good outcome — the same backwards logic as Shapiro-Wilk.

p = 0.924 — nothing to worry about.

Tip

🖐 Levene vs Bartlett

Bartlett’s test is more powerful but assumes normality itself. Levene’s works on absolute deviations from the group median, so it is far more robust. Use Levene.

Shapiro-Wilk — Normality of the Residuals

shapiro.test(residuals(crab_model))

    Shapiro-Wilk normality test

data:  residuals(crab_model)
W = 0.97566, p-value = 0.112

Note what goes inside: residuals(crab_model).

p = 0.112 — the residuals are consistent with normal.

Warning

⚠️ Do not let the test overrule the plot

At small n it cannot detect real problems; at large n it flags departures too small to matter. The QQ plot is the primary evidence.

One Call for All of It

performance::check_model() draws every assumption check in one labelled panel, with a plain-English caption on each.

check_model(crab_model)

Useful for a quick look. Still worth knowing what each panel is, which is why we did them one at a time first.

Part 7 · The Reference Level, Again

R Sorts Alphabetically — Still

Read the file without thinking about it and the four crab types become a factor in alphabetical order:

levels(crab_df$crab_type)
[1] "female"             "intact male"        "male major removed"
[4] "male minor removed"

So the reference is female — and every coefficient in the model becomes “compared with a female crab.”

Warning

⚠️ That is the wrong baseline for this experiment

The question is what happens when you remove a claw from a male. The comparison that answers it is against an intact male, not against a female.

The model fits identically either way. The output only answers your question if you tell it what your question was.

🗳️ DECIDE · Which Crab Is the Control?

You are about to set the reference level deliberately.

Which level should it be, and why?

(a) female — keep R’s default; the model fit is identical anyway

(b) intact male — the untouched version of the animals that were manipulated

(c) male minor removed — it is the procedural control, so it accounts for the surgery

(d) It makes no difference, because the F-test and R² do not change

Warning

✍️ Careful — two of these are true statements. Only one is the right decision.

Note

The question to ask

Which single comparison do I most want to read straight off the coefficient table?

fct_relevel() · Saying What You Meant

Important

✅ The answer is (b).

  1. is true — F, R², and the ANOVA table are identical whichever level is the reference. That is not a reason to accept a default chosen by spelling.

  2. is tempting and it is the right comparison to make eventually — but as the baseline it hides the very contrast the experiment was built around.

crab_df <- crab_df %>%
  mutate(crab_type = fct_relevel(crab_type, "intact male"))

crab_model <- lm(body_temp_c ~ crab_type, data = crab_df)

summary(crab_model)$coefficients %>% round(3)

Now every row reads as “degrees hotter than an intact male”:

  • female +0.39 — clearly hotter

Tip

🖐 The habit

Set the reference in the same line that creates the factor. fct_relevel(x, "control") moves one level to the front and leaves the rest alone.

Part 8 · Post-Hoc Testing

What Post-Hoc Tests Are For

The F-test said something differs. Post-hoc tests say what, while keeping the family-wide error rate at α.

  • Planned (a priori) comparisons — a small number of contrasts you committed to before seeing the data. More powerful, because there are fewer of them
  • Unplanned (post-hoc) comparisons — all pairs, after the fact. Safer, less powerful

Tukey’s HSD is the standard for all pairwise comparisons with equal n.

Warning

⚠️ Deciding after you look is not a plan

Choosing which comparisons to make because they looked big re-introduces exactly the multiple-comparisons problem the correction exists to solve.

🗳️ DECIDE · Reading the Tukey Output

The radiator hypothesis predicts that a male whose major claw was removed should run hotter than an intact male.

Tukey gives male major removed − intact male a difference of +0.129 with an adjusted p = 0.196.

What should you conclude?

(a) The hypothesis is supported — the difference is in the predicted direction

(b) The hypothesis fails — removing the major claw did not significantly change temperature

(c) The hypothesis is supported, because females are significantly hotter than every male group

(d) Nothing can be concluded, because the overall ANOVA was significant

Warning

✍️ One letter, then argue it with your neighbour.

Note

The question to ask

Which comparison does the hypothesis actually make a prediction about?

What Tukey Actually Found

Important

✅ The answer is (b).

Removing the major claw moved temperature by 0.13 °C, adjusted p = 0.2 — not distinguishable from zero. The direction is right and the evidence is not there.

(c) is the trap: females are hotter than every male group, but females differ from males in many ways besides the claw. That comparison cannot test a claw-removal hypothesis.

Tip

🖐 Why emmeans and not just TukeyHSD()

TukeyHSD() only does all-pairs on a balanced aov. emmeans handles unbalanced designs, covariates, several factors and planned contrasts — and it is what you will use from Week 9 onward.

Part 9 · Reporting

Reporting an ANOVA

A results sentence needs the test, the statistic with both df, the p-value, and the direction — then the post-hoc detail.

Body temperature differed among crab types (one-way ANOVA, F3,80 = 20.31, p < 0.001, R² = 0.43). Females were warmer than intact males by 0.39 °C (Tukey HSD, adjusted p < 0.001), but removing the major claw did not detectably change male temperature (0.13 °C, adjusted p = 0.2).

Note

✅ What that sentence does

  • Names the test
  • Gives both degrees of freedom
  • Reports effect size, not just p
  • States the post-hoc method and that its p-values are adjusted
  • Reports the non-significant comparison the hypothesis was about, rather than quietly dropping it

Part 10 · When the Assumptions Fail

🗳️ DECIDE · What Do You Do Now?

An ANOVA on these data returns F = 15.25, p = 5.87e-09 — but the residuals are non-normal and the variances are unequal.

What is the right next step?

(a) Report the ANOVA — it is significant, and ANOVA is robust

(b) Drop the group with the biggest variance and re-run

(c) Try a transformation, and if that fails use a rank-based test such as Kruskal-Wallis

(d) Report nothing — the data cannot be analysed

Warning

✍️ Write your letter down, then vote.

Note

The question to ask

Is the problem with the data, or with the test I chose for it?

The Kruskal-Wallis Test

Important

✅ The answer is (c).

Transform first — a log or square root often fixes both problems at once, and keeps you in a framework that extends to more factors. If it does not, move to a rank-based test.

(b) is data deletion to make a test pass, which is not a repair.

kruskal.test(lifespan_days ~ treatment, data = worm_df)

    Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test

data:  lifespan_days by treatment
Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 29.463, df = 3, p-value = 1.79e-06

Kruskal-Wallis replaces every value by its rank and asks whether the rank totals differ more than chance — so it needs no normality and is not thrown by unequal spread.

χ² = 29.46, df = 3, p = 1.79e-06.

The conclusion survives. What changes is that you can now defend it.

Dunn’s Test — the Post-Hoc for Ranks

Kruskal-Wallis has the same limit as F: it says something differs, not what. Dunn’s test is its post-hoc.

dunnTest(lifespan_days ~ treatment,
         data = worm_df, method = "bh")$res %>%
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), ~ signif(.x, 3)))
           Comparison      Z  P.unadj    P.adj
1       water - adult -3.120 1.78e-03 2.67e-03
2       water - larva  0.917 3.59e-01 4.31e-01
3       adult - larva  4.040 5.32e-05 1.60e-04
4 water - larva+adult -3.510 4.56e-04 9.11e-04
5 adult - larva+adult -0.381 7.03e-01 7.03e-01
6 larva - larva+adult -4.420 9.76e-06 5.85e-05
Note

Which adjustment?

"bonferroni" is the strictest and costs the most power. "bh" (Benjamini-Hochberg) controls the false discovery rate instead, and is the usual choice when you have more than a handful of comparisons.

pairwise.wilcox.test() does a similar job; Dunn’s is preferred because it uses the same ranking as the Kruskal-Wallis it follows.

Which Test Should You Use?

Situation Parametric route Non-parametric route
Compare 2 groups t-test Mann-Whitney U / Wilcoxon rank-sum
Compare 3+ groups one-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis
Post-hoc, all pairs Tukey HSD, emmeans Dunn’s test, pairwise Wilcoxon
What it compares means mean ranks (≈ medians)
Needs normal residuals, equal variance neither
Costs you nothing, when assumptions hold power, and the original units

Try a transformation before you give up the parametric route — it keeps your effect sizes interpretable and extends to the multi-factor designs coming in Week 9.

Reporting a Non-Parametric Result

Nematode lifespan differed among parasite-exposure treatments (Kruskal-Wallis χ²3 = 29.46, p < 0.001). A Kruskal-Wallis test was used because the residuals of the equivalent ANOVA were non-normal (Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.00181) and variances were unequal (Levene p = 1.49e-12). Dunn’s tests with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment showed that larva-exposed worms outlived worms exposed as adults or as both.

Important

✅ Say why you went non-parametric

A reader cannot tell whether you chose a rank test because your data required it or because it gave you the answer you wanted — unless you show the assumption checks that made the decision.

Report the numbers that drove the choice, not just the choice.

The Three Things to Carry Out of Here

Important

⚠️ A significant F is an entry ticket

It says at least one group differs. It never says which, how many, or in which direction. Everything past that comes from post-hoc comparisons you planned or corrected for.

Note

✅ Assumptions live in the residuals

Except independence, which lives in the design — and no plot will ever show it to you.

Important

✅ Set the reference level

fct_relevel(x, "control"), in the line that makes the factor. The fit never changes; the readability always does.

Note

Up next — Two-Way ANOVA

Two factors at once, and the thing that only exists when you have two: the interaction.

Getting unstuck

When code breaks — and it will, that is normal:

  1. Read the error message out loud; it usually names the line
  2. Check the usual suspects: is your grouping column a factor? Did you pass residuals(model) to shapiro.test() rather than the raw response? Is car loaded for leveneTest(), FSA for dunnTest()?
  3. ?function_name opens the help page
  4. Bring the exact error (copy-paste it) to class or office hours
Note

✅ Key idea

Every working scientist googles error messages daily. Getting stuck is not failing — it is the job.
