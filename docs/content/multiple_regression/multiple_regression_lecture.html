The Study · Birds in Fragmented Forest

Loyn (1987) surveyed 56 remnant forest patches in Victoria, Australia, and counted the birds in each one. For every patch he also recorded six things that might explain the count.

Small patches, isolated patches, recently cut-off patches and heavily grazed patches are all candidates — and they are not independent of each other.

grazing grazing intensity, 1–5

When Do We Need More Than One Predictor?

Bird abundance in a patch plausibly depends on all seven of those things at once, and they are not independent of each other — small patches tend to be grazed harder, and isolated patches tend to be older.

Fitting one predictor at a time cannot answer “does area matter once grazing is accounted for.”

  • Predictors all continuous → multiple regression
  • A mix of continuous and categorical → still multiple regression, with dummy variables

Part 2 · The Multiple Regression Model

The Multiple Regression Model

For i = 1 to n observations and p predictors:

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \epsilon_i\]

  • \(y_i\) — the response for observation i
  • \(\beta_0\) — the intercept, the mean of Y when every predictor is 0
  • \(\beta_1 \dots \beta_p\) — the partial regression slopes
  • \(\epsilon_i\) — the error, estimated by the residual \(y_i - \hat{y}_i\)
Note

✅ Key idea — what “partial” means

A partial slope is the change in Y per one-unit change in that predictor while every other predictor is held constant.

It is a conditional statement. Change what else is in the model and you have changed the condition — so the number is allowed to change too.

Why Not Just Regress Y on Each X Separately?

Each simple regression asks “does this predictor track abundance?” The multiple regression asks “does it track abundance among patches that are otherwise alike?”

Those are different questions, and they can have different answers.

Predictor Alone In the full model
log area 9.78 7.47
grazing -4.98 -1.67
year isolated 0.211 0.074
altitude 0.095 0.02
Warning

⚠️ Neither number is “wrong”

If you want to predict bird abundance from grazing alone, -4.98 is the right slope to use.

If you want to claim grazing has an effect beyond the fact that grazed patches are small, -1.67 is the number you have to defend — and here you cannot.

Estimation and Prediction

Estimation. Ordinary least squares again: choose the coefficients that minimise \(\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\). With several predictors this is a set of simultaneous equations solved with matrix algebra — lm() does it instantly, and what matters is knowing what it minimises.

Prediction. Substitute new values for every predictor.

  • More observations → narrower intervals
  • More predictors → wider intervals
  • Stay inside the range of every predictor
Warning

⚠️ Extrapolation is worse here

In simple regression you only have to stay inside one range. Here you have to stay inside the data cloud — and a combination can be far outside it even when each predictor separately looks ordinary.

There are no 1000-ha patches under intense grazing in these data. The model will still happily predict one.

Part 3 · ANOVA for Multiple Regression

Partitioning Variance — Sums of Squares

Exactly the partition from last week, with p predictors instead of one:

\[SS_{total} = SS_{regression} + SS_{residual}\]

  • \(SS_{regression}\) — variation the model explains
  • \(SS_{residual}\) — variation it does not
  • Regression df is now p, not 1
Source SS df MS
Regression \(\sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2\) \(p\) SS/df
Residual \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) \(n-p-1\) SS/df
Total \(\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\) \(n-1\)

Mean squares are variances, so they do not grow with n — which is why the F-ratio is built from them and not from the sums of squares directly.

Hypotheses — the “Basic” Test

The overall F-test at the bottom of summary() tests one null:

\[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_p = 0\]

  • If it is true, \(MS_{regression}\) and \(MS_{residual}\) estimate the same variance and F ≈ 1
  • If any β is non-zero, \(MS_{regression}\) is inflated and F > 1

It tells you the model as a whole explains something. It does not tell you which predictor did the work.

Warning

⚠️ A significant overall F with nothing significant inside it

This happens, and it is the classic signature of collinearity: the predictors jointly explain a lot, but they are so tangled that no individual slope can be pinned down.

Hypotheses — Testing One Predictor

To ask whether one specific β is zero, fit the model with and without it and compare:

\[SS_{extra} = SS_{regression}^{full} - SS_{regression}^{reduced}\]

\[F_{1,\,n-p-1} = \frac{MS_{extra}}{MS_{residual}^{full}}\]

reduced_model <- lm(abundance ~ log_area + grazing,
                    data = bird_df)

anova(reduced_model, full_model)

Explained Variance

\[R^2 = \frac{SS_{regression}}{SS_{total}} = 1 - \frac{SS_{residual}}{SS_{total}}\]

For the reduced model: \(\dfrac{4137}{6338}\) = 0.653

The six-predictor model manages 0.685 — higher, as it must be.

Warning

⚠️ Watch out

  • R² always rises when you add a predictor, even a useless one
  • So R² cannot be used to compare models with different numbers of predictors
  • R² from models with differently transformed Y are not comparable at all

That is why adjusted R² and AIC exist, and we come back to them in Part 8.

Part 4 · Assumptions and Diagnostics

First: Does a Predictor Need a Transform?

Assumptions and Diagnostics — Outliers

  • Fixed X values — unrealistic in field data, but the working assumption
  • No overly influential points — check leverage and influence with Cook’s distance
  • A point can be extreme in Y, extreme in X, or extreme in the combination; only the third kind is uniquely a multiple-regression problem

Largest Cook’s distance here is 0.2 — nothing near the usual threshold of 1.

Part 5 · Collinearity

Collinearity — the Problem

Predictors in field data are almost never independent. Here, small patches are grazed harder — r = -0.56.

When predictors overlap:

  • Parameter estimates become unstable — one changed value can move them a lot
  • Standard errors inflate, so slopes lose significance
  • The model can fit well while no single slope is trustworthy

Collinearity — Detecting It

Variance Inflation Factors:

\[VIF_{X_j} = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_j}\]

where \(R^2_j\) comes from regressing \(X_j\) on all the other predictors — not from a pairwise correlation. (With two predictors they coincide; with more they do not.)

  • Rule of thumb: VIF > 10 is bad; some authors use > 5
  • Simplest fix: drop predictors that are measuring the same thing
vif(full_model)
       log_area        log_dist log_dist_larger   year_isolated         grazing 
       1.911514        1.654553        2.009749        1.804769        2.524814 
     altitude_m 
       1.467937
Note

✅ Key idea

A high VIF does not mean the model is wrong. It means you cannot trust the individual partial slopes, even when the model as a whole fits well.

What the Data Can and Cannot Separate

Important

✅ The answer is (c).

Grazing and area are correlated at r = -0.56. The heavily grazed patches in this dataset are also the small ones, so no analysis can tell you which of the two is doing the work.

(a) overstates it — a non-significant partial slope is not evidence of no effect. (b) is the common mistake: the full model is not “better”, it answers a different, conditional question. Tip

🖐 What would settle it

Find, or create, patches that break the correlation: large heavily grazed patches and small lightly grazed ones.

That is a design problem, not an analysis problem — which is where this course started.

Part 6 · Interactions

Interactions — Additive vs. Multiplicative

So far every model has been additive — the effect of area is the same at every grazing level.

An interaction says the effect of one predictor depends on the level of another:

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_{1} + \beta_2X_{2} + \epsilon_i\] \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_{1} + \beta_2X_{2} + \beta_3X_{1}X_{2} + \epsilon_i\]

An interaction term is just another predictor: the product of two others, with its own coefficient.

Note

✅ Key idea

Additive: parallel lines.

Interactive: lines with different slopes — the fitted surface is no longer flat.

Interactions — Fitting One

interaction_model <- lm(abundance ~ log_area * grazing,
                        data = bird_df)

summary(interaction_model)$coefficients
                  Estimate Std. Error   t value     Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept)      26.558079  3.3576293  7.909771 1.768256e-10
log_area          2.112005  2.0375755  1.036528 3.047520e-01
grazing          -4.547139  0.8850592 -5.137667 4.246998e-06
log_area:grazing  2.142303  0.7358161  2.911465 5.287173e-03

The interaction is significant (p = 0.00529), and a * b expands to a + b + a:b automatically.

What it means: patch area matters more where grazing is heavy. In lightly grazed country a small patch still holds birds; under heavy grazing only the big patches do.

Warning

⚠️ You cannot read the main effects the same way any more

With an interaction in the model, the log_area coefficient is the slope when grazing = 0 — a value that does not exist in these data.

Interactions — the Cost

  • Parameter proliferation. Every predictor you add roughly doubles the number of possible terms — six predictors with all interactions is 64 terms, and you have 56 patches
  • Interpretation gets harder, as the slide before shows
  • Include an interaction when it makes biological sense, not because it might be significant

“Does fragmentation hurt more where grazing is heavy” is a real hypothesis. That is why this one belongs.

Warning

⚠️ Watch out

Test enough interactions and one will reach p < 0.05 by chance. With six predictors there are 15 two-way interactions alone — at α = 0.05 you expect roughly one false positive just from testing them all.

Part 7 · Factors and Dummy Variables

Categorical Predictors Become Dummy Variables

So far grazing has been a number, 1 to 5, which forces every step to have the same effect. That is a strong assumption — is the jump from light to moderate really the same size as moderate to heavy?

Treat it as a category instead and the model estimates each level separately. A categorical predictor with k levels becomes k − 1 dummy variables, each 0 or 1.

One level gets no dummy at all. That is the reference level, and every other coefficient is a comparison against it.

count(bird_df, grazing, grazing_class)

Choosing the Reference Yourself · fct_relevel()

Important

✅ The answer is (c).

  1. is true — the fit, R², F and the ANOVA table are all identical whichever level is the reference. But it is not a reason to accept a bad default: the coefficients are what people read, and they should answer the question you asked.

minimal grazing is the closest thing this observational study has to a control, so putting it first makes every other coefficient “birds lost relative to the least-grazed patches.”

bird_df <- bird_df %>%
  mutate(grazing_class = fct_relevel(
    grazing_class,
    "minimal", "light", "moderate", "heavy", "intense"))

levels(bird_df$grazing_class)
[1] "minimal"  "light"    "moderate" "heavy"    "intense"
Tip

🖐 The habit to build

Any time a factor enters a model, set its reference level on purpose, in the same line where you create the factor.

fct_relevel() moves the levels you name to the front and leaves the rest in place, so naming just the control is enough:

fct_relevel(treatment, "control")

Now every coefficient is a comparison with minimal grazing, and the pattern is obvious:

  • light, moderate and heavy are all indistinguishable from minimal
  • intense costs about 11.9 birds, p < 0.001

Grazing is a threshold, not a gradient — and fitting it as the number 1–5 hid that completely, averaging the effect into -2.85 birds per step.

Why This Matters Even More in ANOVA

With only continuous predictors there is no reference level at all — every slope is “per unit of X” and nothing is being compared with anything.

The moment a factor enters the model, every coefficient becomes a contrast against the reference, and that is the whole structure of the next five weeks:

  • One-way ANOVA — treatment means against a control
  • Two-way ANOVA — main effects read at the other factor’s reference level
  • ANCOVA — separate intercepts, all relative to one group
Important

✅ The rule to carry forward

factor() gives you alphabetical order. Alphabetical order is almost never the order your experiment was designed in.

Set the control as the first level, every time, and your model output reads as “what each treatment did to the control” without any further work.

Part 8 · Comparing Models

Comparing Models — the Problem

  • Which predictors should be in the model?
  • Occam’s razor: the best model balances fit against complexity
  • Overfitting: a high R² bought with useless predictors — which is why R² alone cannot choose between models

Compare nested models: one whose predictors are a subset of the other’s.

Note

📖 Reference

Quinn & Keough, Ch. 6, works through exactly this dataset. Whitlock & Schluter, Ch. 17, covers model comparison for regression.

Comparing Models — Penalizing Complexity

Two ways to charge a model for its predictors:

\[\text{Adjusted } R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{Residual}/(n - p - 1)}{SS_{Total}/(n - 1)}\]

\[\text{AIC} = n\ln(SS_{Residual}) + 2(p + 1) - n\ln(n)\]

  • Higher adjusted R² is better
  • Lower AIC is better
  • AIC is comparative only — the number on its own means nothing, and models must be fitted to the same rows
Warning

⚠️ The NA trap

If one predictor has missing values, lm() silently drops those rows. Two models fitted to different numbers of rows cannot be compared by AIC, and R will not warn you.

Comparing Models — How to Search

  • Fit all possible models and compare — fine with a few predictors, hopeless with many
  • Automated stepwise selection adds or removes terms by partial F or AIC
  • We use manual backward selection: start full, drop what earns nothing, stop when dropping hurts
Warning

⚠️ Stepwise selection is controversial

It inflates R², biases p-values, and knows nothing about biology. A small set of models you can justify beats an automated search over all of them.

Comparing Models — Putting It Together

model_comparison <- tibble(

Part 9 · Relative Importance of Predictors

How Important Is Each Predictor?

Three standard approaches, and they answer slightly different questions:

  1. F- or t-tests on the partial slopes
  2. Coefficient of partial determination — the variance a predictor adds
  3. Standardized partial slopes — every predictor in SD units

None of them is “the” right answer.

Warning

⚠️ Why the raw slopes cannot be compared

log_area has a slope of 6.89 and grazing -2.85.

One is “birds per tenfold increase in area,” the other is “birds per step of grazing intensity.” Comparing those two numbers is comparing metres with kilograms.

Partial η² = \(\dfrac{SS_{predictor}}{SS_{predictor} + SS_{residual}}\) — variance explained after controlling for the other predictor.

log_area grazing
partial η² 0.35 0.232
ΔR² 0.187 0.105

ΔR² is what the model’s R² would lose if you dropped that predictor — its unique contribution.

Both say area is the stronger of the two.

Approach 3 — Standardized Partial Slopes

Rescale every variable to mean 0 and SD 1, and the slopes become directly comparable: “SDs of Y per SD of X.”

bird_z <- bird_df %>%
  mutate(across(c(abundance, log_area, grazing),
                ~ as.numeric(scale(.))))

lm(abundance ~ log_area + grazing, data = bird_z) %>%
  coef() %>% round(3)
(Intercept)    log_area     grazing 
      0.000       0.521      -0.391

log_area 0.52 versus grazing -0.39 — area is about 1.3× the influence of grazing per standard deviation.

Note

✅ All three agree here

Partial F, partial R² and standardized slopes all rank area above grazing. When they disagree, that is worth reporting — it usually means the predictors are tangled.

The Three Things to Carry Out of Here

Important

⚠️ R² always rises

Adding predictors can only increase plain R². Adjusted R² and AIC exist precisely because “explains more variance” is not the same as “is a better model.”

Note

✅ A partial slope is conditional

“What does area do among patches grazed the same amount?” Change what else is in the model and you have changed the question.

Important

✅ Set your reference level

factor() sorts alphabetically. Your control is almost never first alphabetically — use fct_relevel() and make the output say what you meant.

This is the habit that makes every ANOVA from Week 8 onward readable.

Getting unstuck

When code breaks — and it will, that is normal:

  1. Read the error message out loud; it usually names the line
  2. Check the usual suspects: is it y ~ x1 + x2 (additive) or y ~ x1 * x2 (interaction)? Is car loaded for vif() and Anova()? Are you comparing models fitted to the same rows — an NA in one predictor silently drops that row
  3. ?function_name opens the help page
  4. Bring the exact error (copy-paste it) to class or office hours
Note

✅ Key idea

Every working scientist googles error messages daily. Getting stuck is not failing — it is the job.
