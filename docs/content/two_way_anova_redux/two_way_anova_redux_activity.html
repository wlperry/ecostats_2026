Activity: Two-Way ANOVA Redux

The same machinery on data that fights back

Hands-on activity: a second factorial ANOVA on the genuinely unbalanced Sargent & Lodge crayfish growth data — reading Anova(type = 3) and summary() line by line, recovering the coefficients contr.sum does not print, comparing Type I/II/III where they actually differ, and using emmeans four ways to localise an interaction.
Author

Bill Perry

In-class Activity 14: Two-Way ANOVA Redux

Today’s Objectives

  1. Measure how unbalanced a design is, and say why proportionality matters
  2. Read Anova(model, type = 3) completely — every row, every df
  3. Read summary(model) line by line, and recover the coefficients contr.sum does not print
  4. Rebuild a cell mean by hand from the coefficients and match emmeans
  5. Compare Type I, II and III on a design where they disagree
  6. Use emmeans four ways: marginal, simple effects both directions, interaction contrast
  7. Decide whether a failed assumption changes the conclusion

🧩 Block 1 — The design, and how broken it is (after lecture Parts 1–2)

Sections 1–2: meet the data, then measure the imbalance.

Section 1 · The data

Written for you in the skeleton — run it and read it.

# ---- Libraries -------------------------------------------
library(car)        # Anova(), leveneTest()
library(emmeans)    # emmeans(), emmip(), contrast()
library(effectsize) # eta_squared()
library(janitor)    # clean_names()
library(tidyverse)
# ---- 1 The data ------------------------------------------
cray_df <- read_csv("data/sargent_lodge_crayfish.csv") %>%
  clean_names() %>%
  mutate(range = factor(range, levels = c("Native", "Invasive")),
         lake  = factor(lake,  levels = c("Big", "High", "Papoose")))

head(cray_df)

cray_cells <- cray_df %>%
  group_by(range, lake) %>%
  summarise(n = n(),
            mean = mean(growth_per_day),
            sd   = sd(growth_per_day),
            .groups = "drop")

cray_cells

range (2 levels) × lake (3 levels), crossed — a 2 × 3 factorial with six cells and 84 crayfish.

✏️ Q1: Read the mean column in pairs within each lake. Where is the Native–Invasive gap large, and where is it gone?

✏️ Q2: Now read the sd column. Which cell has the smallest spread, which the largest, and roughly what is the ratio?

Section 2 · How unbalanced?

Written for you in the skeleton — this is bookkeeping.

# ---- 2 How unbalanced ------------------------------------
cray_df %>%
  count(range, lake) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = lake, values_from = n)

✏️ Q3: Every cell is filled, so every term is estimable. But is the design proportional — do the cell counts follow the row and column totals? Work out what fraction of the Native crayfish were in Big, and what fraction of the Invasive ones were.

✏️ Q4: Last session you forced the penguins to be balanced by throwing birds away. Why is that a bad idea here?

Now look at it before modelling it.

ggplot(cray_df, aes(lake, growth_per_day, fill = range)) +
  geom_boxplot()

ggplot(cray_cells, aes(lake, mean, color = range, group = range)) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_point()

✏️ Q5: In the second plot, are the lines parallel? Do they cross? Describe in one sentence the shape of what you see.

Before any model, check the design for confounds.

cray_df %>%
  group_by(range) %>%
  summarise(days = paste(sort(unique(days)), collapse = ", "),
            mean_temp = mean(avg_temp),
            n = n())

✏️ Q6: days and range are perfectly confounded. The response is growth per day, so what does that fix — and what does it not fix? Which of the three ANOVA terms are unaffected by this problem?

Section 4 · Read summary() line by line

YOUR CODE: run summary(cray_model).

✏️ Q10: The design has six cells and the table has six rows. List the coefficient names you expected and did not get. How many are missing?

✏️ Q11: Nothing was dropped. What rule is contr.sum imposing that makes those rows redundant?

Now recover them.

# ---- 4 Recover the missing coefficients ------------------

YOUR CODE: pull b <- coef(cray_model), then build a named vector containing the Native effect, the Invasive effect, the Big and High effects, and the Papoose effect.

✏️ Q12: Which lake has the largest effect in absolute size? Was it printed by summary()?

✏️ Q13: lake1 and lake2 are both positive and significant. Explain why “being in a lake raises growth” is the wrong conclusion to draw from that.

Next, the intercept.

YOUR CODE: compare three numbers — coef(cray_model)[[1]], mean(cray_cells$mean), and mean(cray_df$growth_per_day).

✏️ Q14: Two of those three are identical. Which two, and what does that tell you the intercept actually is? Why would all three have agreed on the penguins’ balanced subsample?

Now the payoff: rebuild a cell mean from the coefficients.

YOUR CODE: compute the fitted mean for Invasive crayfish in Papoose using only b. Every term is a negative, because Invasive is level 2 of range and Papoose is level 3 of lake. Then compare it to emmeans(cray_model, ~ range * lake) and to the observed cell mean.

✏️ Q15: Do all three agree exactly, or just closely? Why does a model with an interaction reproduce the cell means perfectly?

✏️ Q16: summary() gives lake two t-tests and Anova() gives it one F-test. Which would you report to claim “lake matters”, and why is the other one not a substitute?

Section 5 · Type I, II and III

# ---- 5 The three SS types --------------------------------

YOUR CODE: run all three on cray_model — anova() for Type I, Anova(..., type = 2), Anova(..., type = 3). Compare the three F values for each of the three terms.

✏️ Q17: Which term’s F changes most between Type I and Type III? By how much?

✏️ Q18: The interaction F is identical in all three. Why must that always be true?

Now show that Type I depends on typing order.

YOUR CODE: fit the model again as growth_per_day ~ lake * range and pull range’s Type I sum of squares from each of the two fits.

✏️ Q19: You got two different sums of squares for the same term from the same data. Which one is “right”?

✏️ Q20: Which type would you report here, and what is the one-sentence justification? (Think about whether either factor is prior to the other.)

🧩 Block 3 — emmeans four ways, then assumptions (after lecture Parts 6–7)

Sections 6–7: unpack the interaction, then decide whether you can trust it.

Section 6 · emmeans, four ways

# ---- 6 emmeans, four ways --------------------------------

Way 1 — marginal means.

YOUR CODE: emmeans(cray_model, ~ range). Then compute the raw mean of growth_per_day by range with group_by() and compare.

✏️ Q21: The EMM difference is larger than the raw difference. Which direction is the raw mean biased, and which cells caused it?

✏️ Q22: R printed a NOTE above the table. What is it warning you about, and is it right?

Way 2 — simple effects of range within each lake.

YOUR CODE: pairs(emmeans(cray_model, ~ range | lake)).

✏️ Q23: Write the result of the study in one sentence using these three numbers.

Way 3 — simple effects of lake within each range.

YOUR CODE: pairs(emmeans(cray_model, ~ lake | range)).

✏️ Q24: Did lake stop mattering for one of the ranges, or does it matter more for one? What does that tell you about the shape of the interaction?

✏️ Q25: Way 3 shows a Tukey adjustment and Way 2 does not. Why?

Way 4 — the interaction contrast.

YOUR CODE: contrast(emmeans(cray_model, ~ range * lake), interaction = c("pairwise", "pairwise")).

✏️ Q26: These are differences of differences. Which lake is responsible for the interaction, and which two lakes behave the same?

✏️ Q27: Of the four ways, only one tells you where in the design the interaction lives. Which, and why can the Anova() table never do that?

Finally, the picture.

emmip(cray_model, range ~ lake, CIs = TRUE)

✏️ Q28: Where do the confidence bands overlap, and where do they not? Does the figure agree with Way 4?

Section 7 · Effect size and assumptions

# ---- 7 Effect size and assumptions -----------------------

YOUR CODE: eta_squared(Anova(cray_model, type = 3)).

✏️ Q29: Which term has the largest effect size? Was it the one the study was about? What does that suggest about how to design the next experiment?

YOUR CODE: check the assumptions — shapiro.test(residuals(cray_model)), leveneTest(growth_per_day ~ range * lake, data = cray_df), and a plot of rstandard() against fitted().

✏️ Q30: Both tests fail. Look at the cell sd column from section 1 again — is the spread random, or does it track the cell means?

Now find out whether it matters.

YOUR CODE: refit on log(growth_per_day), run Anova(..., type = 3) on it, and re-run shapiro.test and leveneTest on the log model.

✏️ Q31: Did the log transform fix the assumptions? Did it change any conclusion?

✏️ Q32: You now have two analyses that agree. Which do you report, and what one extra sentence do you owe the reader?
