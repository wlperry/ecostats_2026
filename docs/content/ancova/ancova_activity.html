Activity: ANCOVA

Covariates

Hands-on activity: analysis of covariance on Partridge & Farquhar’s fruitfly longevity data — testing homogeneity of slopes, what a covariate buys you in power, and adjusted means — then Arctic grayling allometry, where the slopes are not parallel and standard ANCOVA is off the table.
Author

Bill Perry

In-class Activity 17: ANCOVA

Today’s Objectives

  1. Say what a covariate is, and the two different jobs it does
  2. Test homogeneity of slopes, and know why it comes first
  3. Fit an additive ANCOVA and read its ANOVA table
  4. Measure what the covariate bought you — in residual SS and in F
  5. Read adjusted means, and say how they differ from raw means
  6. Recognise heterogeneous slopes and stop before fitting the wrong model
  7. Use emtrends() to compare slopes, and contrast groups at covariate values
  8. Say where an ANCOVA adjustment becomes extrapolation

Where this fits

Week 10 changed the distribution. Week 11 changed the error structure. Today changes neither — it goes back to the ordinary linear model and asks what happens when you have one categorical predictor and one continuous predictor at the same time.

That is an ANCOVA, and it is the model that joins the ANOVA half of this course to the regression half.

What it is Example today
Response continuous fruitfly longevity, days
Factor categorical, the thing you care about mating treatment
Covariate continuous, the thing you adjust for thorax length, mm

A covariate does two different jobs, and they are worth keeping separate in your head:

Job What goes wrong without it Fixed by
Confounding groups sit at different covariate values, so a raw comparison of means confuses the two comparing at a common covariate value
Noise the covariate’s variation sits in the error term, shrinking your F removing it from the residual
Important

The one rule. The covariate must be measured independently of the treatment and must not be affected by it. Adjusting for something your treatment caused deletes the effect you are trying to measure. No diagnostic will catch this — only knowing how the data were collected.

🧩 Block 1 — The data and the critical test (after lecture Parts 1–3)

Sections 1–2: meet the fruitflies, then run the one test that decides everything else.

Section 1 · The fruitfly data

Written for you in the skeleton — run it and read it.

# ---- Libraries -------------------------------------------
library(janitor)
library(car)          # Anova()
library(emmeans)      # emmeans(), emtrends()
library(broom)
library(lmtest)       # bptest()
library(interactions) # sim_slopes()
library(tidyverse)

options(scipen = 999)
options(contrasts = c("contr.sum", "contr.poly"))  # for Anova(type = 3)

Partridge & Farquhar (1981) asked whether sexual activity shortens a male fruitfly’s life. They housed 125 males, 25 in each of five treatments, and recorded how long each lived.

The design is cleverer than it first looks:

Treatment Females Do they mate?
No females 0 —
One inseminated female daily 1 no — newly inseminated females will not re-mate
Eight inseminated females daily 8 no
One virgin female daily 1 yes
Eight virgin females daily 8 yes 
# ---- 1 The fruitfly data ---------------------------------
p_df <- read_csv("data/partridge.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) %>%
  clean_names() %>%
  mutate(
    treat = factor(
      case_when(
        partners == 0               ~ "no females",
        partners == 1 & type == 0   ~ "1 inseminated",
        partners == 8 & type == 0   ~ "8 inseminated",
        partners == 1 & type == 1   ~ "1 virgin",
        partners == 8 & type == 1   ~ "8 virgin"
      ),
      levels = c("no females", "1 inseminated", "8 inseminated",
                 "1 virgin", "8 virgin")
    )
  )

head(p_df)

count(p_df, treat)
Warning

⚠️ Why the labels are built from partners and type, not from treatmen

The file has a treatmen column coded 1–5, but the codes are not in the order you would guess: 1 is eight inseminated, 2 is no females, 3 is one inseminated, 4 is one virgin, 5 is eight virgin.

Labelling them 1→“none”, 2→“one virgin”, 3→“eight virgin” — the obvious guess — reverses the biological conclusion. Building the labels from the design columns (partners and type) instead means they cannot drift out of step with the data.

✏️ Q1: What is the response, what is the factor, and what is the covariate?

✏️ Q2: Why are the inseminated-female groups in this experiment at all? What would you be unable to conclude without them?

✏️ Q3: Thorax length is the covariate. Could the mating treatment have changed a fly’s thorax length? Why does the answer matter?

Section 2 · Look first, then test the slopes

▶ Type this yourself:

🔮 Predict first: look at those five lines. Are they parallel? Commit to an answer before you run the test.

▶ Type this yourself — the homogeneity-of-slopes test:

slopes_model <- lm(longev ~ thorax * treat, data = p_df)

Anova(slopes_model, type = 3)

✏️ Q4: Write down the F and p for the thorax:treat interaction. Are the slopes homogeneous?

✏️ Q5: This is a test you want to fail to reject. Name one other test you have run this semester where that was also true.

✏️ Q6: Suppose it had come back p = 0.001. What would you do next, and what would you not be allowed to report?

✏️ Q7: Look at the treat row in this table. Is it significant? Hold onto that — you are about to fit a different model and get a very different answer.

💡 Key idea: the interaction term asks whether the groups share one slope. If they do not, there is no single “difference between the groups” to report — the difference depends on where you are along the covariate. That is why this test comes first, before the model you intend to publish.

🧩 Block 2 — The additive model (after lecture Part 4)

Sections 3–5: fit it, measure what the covariate bought, then read the adjusted means.

Section 3 · Fit the ANCOVA

Slopes are homogeneous, so drop the interaction.

▶ Type this yourself:

# ---- 3 Fit the ANCOVA ------------------------------------
ancova_model <- lm(longev ~ thorax + treat, data = p_df)

summary(ancova_model)

Anova(ancova_model, type = 2)

✏️ Q8: Both thorax and treat are significant now. In Q7 the treat row was not. Same data, same question — what changed?

✏️ Q9: The thorax coefficient is about 136. What are its units, and what does it mean in fruitflies?

✏️ Q10: summary() shows treat1, treat2, treat3, treat4 — four coefficients for five groups. Why four, and what is the fifth group’s effect?

▶ Type this yourself — check the assumptions on the final model:

par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
plot(ancova_model)
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

shapiro.test(residuals(ancova_model))

bptest(ancova_model)

✏️ Q11: Do the diagnostics look acceptable? Which plot tells you about equal variances?

Section 4 · What did the covariate buy you?

This is the whole power argument, in two models.

🔮 Predict first: run the same question with no covariate at all. Will the treatment effect get stronger, weaker, or stay the same?

▶ Type this yourself:

# ---- 4 What did the covariate buy you? -------------------
no_cov_model <- lm(longev ~ treat, data = p_df)

Anova(no_cov_model, type = 2)

Anova(ancova_model, type = 2)

no covariate
ANCOVA

✏️ Q13: What fraction of the residual sum of squares did one degree of freedom of thorax remove?

✏️ Q14: The treatment effect was significant both ways. So was the covariate worth including? Give a reason that is not about this particular p-value.

✏️ Q15: The five groups have almost identical mean thorax lengths (check with group_by(treat) %>% summarise(mean(thorax))). Given that, which of the covariate’s two jobs — confounding or noise — is it doing here?

💡 Key idea: a covariate earns its place even when your groups are perfectly balanced on it. Balance kills the confounding problem; it does nothing about the noise problem. Measure the covariate anyway.

Section 5 · Adjusted means

▶ Type this yourself:

# ---- 5 Adjusted means ------------------------------------
p_emm <- emmeans(ancova_model, "treat")
p_emm

p_df %>%
  group_by(treat) %>%
  summarise(raw_mean = mean(longev), mean_thorax = mean(thorax))

▶ Type this yourself — the comparisons:

pairs(p_emm, adjust = "tukey")

multcomp::cld(p_emm, Letters = letters)

plot(p_emm, comparisons = TRUE)

✏️ Q16: Put the adjusted means next to the raw means. How far apart are they, and why is the gap small here?

✏️ Q17: What covariate value are these means adjusted to? (Check mean(p_df$thorax).)

✏️ Q18: Read the letter display. Which groups form one block, and what do those groups have in common biologically?

✏️ Q19: Say the result in one sentence about fruitflies — not about F and p. What exactly is costing them their lifespan?

✏️ Q20: Four of the ten pairwise comparisons are not significant. Which four, and does that pattern support or undercut the conclusion in Q19?

💡 Key idea: adjusted means are what ANCOVA compares — every group’s predicted response at the same covariate value. When the groups are already balanced on the covariate, they land close to the raw means and the covariate is pure power. When the groups are not balanced, the two can differ a lot, and the raw means are the misleading ones.

🧩 Block 3 — When the slopes are not parallel (after lecture Part 5)

Sections 6–7: the same workflow on data that refuses to cooperate.

Section 6 · Grayling allometry (you start this one)

No code for part of this page. You have just run the first two steps of the workflow once. Do them again.

Back to the Arctic grayling from week 5 — lakes I3 and I8. Fish mass scales with length as a power law, so on log-log axes the relationship is a straight line and its slope is the allometric exponent: mass ∝ lengthb. A slope of 3 means the fish keeps its shape as it grows.

Question: do grayling in the two lakes have the same length–mass relationship?

Written for you in the skeleton:

# ---- 6 Grayling allometry --------------------------------
g_df <- read_csv("data/gray_I3_I8.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) %>%
  clean_names() %>%
  filter(mass_g > 0) %>%
  mutate(log_len = log10(length_mm),
         log_mass = log10(mass_g))

count(g_df, lake)

Now you:

  1. Plot log_mass against log_len, colored by lake, with a line per lake.
  2. Run the homogeneity-of-slopes test — the interaction model, then Anova(type = 3).
  3. Decide whether you are allowed to fit log_mass ~ log_len + lake.

💡 Hint: the only things that change from sections 2–3 are the data frame and the three variable names.

✏️ Q21: What is the F and p for the interaction? What does that forbid you from doing?

✏️ Q22: Fit lm(log_mass ~ log_len + lake) anyway and look at the lake term. It is wildly significant. Why is that number not reportable?

✏️ Q23: Compare the two lakes’ length ranges (group_by(lake) %>% summarise(min(length_mm), max(length_mm))). What does that tell you about where you are allowed to compare them?

Section 7 · Reporting heterogeneous slopes

When slopes differ, the slopes are the result.

▶ Type this yourself:

# ---- 7 Reporting heterogeneous slopes --------------------
g_int <- lm(log_mass ~ log_len * lake, data = g_df)

emtrends(g_int, "lake", var = "log_len")

pairs(emtrends(g_int, "lake", var = "log_len"))

▶ Type this yourself — is each slope different from 3, the isometric value?

test(emtrends(g_int, "lake", var = "log_len"), null = 3)

▶ Type this yourself — where along the covariate do the lakes actually differ?

lens <- log10(c(200, 250, 300))

pairs(emmeans(g_int, ~ lake | log_len, at = list(log_len = lens)))

✏️ Q24: Write down each lake’s slope with its SE. Which one is indistinguishable from 3, and what does isometry mean for a fish?

✏️ Q25: The other slope is below 3. In words: what happens to those fish as they get longer?

✏️ Q26: Look at the contrasts at 200, 250 and 300 mm. At which length are the lakes indistinguishable, and what happens to the gap as fish get bigger?

✏️ Q27: You might have reached for sim_slopes(..., johnson_neyman = TRUE) here. Try it. What does it tell you, and why? (Read the warning carefully.)

✏️ Q28: An additive ANCOVA would have reported one “lake effect.” Name the biological finding it would have thrown away.

💡 Key idea: a significant interaction is not a failed analysis — it is usually the more interesting one. “These groups differ by a constant amount” is a weaker, less informative claim than “these groups differ in how they scale.” When the data offer you the second, do not flatten it into the first.
