INTERPRETING THE RANDOM EFFECT VERSUS A FIXED EFFECT — THE CRAYFISH
==========================================================================

1. WHAT A FIXED EFFECT AND A RANDOM EFFECT EACH ESTIMATE

When lake is fixed, the model estimates a mean growth rate for each of Big, High, and Papoose. Those three means are the answer. Under contr.sum R prints two of them as deviations from the centre and the third is implied. You can test whether the lakes differ, run Tukey, and say "Papoose is 0.10 mm/day below the other two". The conclusion is about those three lakes and no others.

When lake is random, the model estimates one number instead: the variance of lake effects, sigma^2_lake. The three lakes are treated as a sample drawn from a population of lakes, and the question changes from "which lakes differ" to "how much do lakes vary". In the crayfish output that number is 0.00396, which is more readable as its square root, SD 0.063 mm/day. The sentence it supports is "a typical lake sits about 0.06 mm/day above or below the average lake". There is no F, no df, no p on that row. The lake is described, not tested.

The practical difference students feel is the parameter count. Three fixed lakes cost two parameters. Three random lakes cost one. Sixteen fixed patches cost fifteen; sixteen random patches still cost one. That is why mixed models scale to designs ANOVA cannot.

2. HOW TO READ THE RANDOM-EFFECT ROW AGAINST THE RESIDUAL ROW

The random-effects block always has at least two rows and they are meant to be compared. For the crayfish:

  row        variance   SD      means
  lake       0.00396    0.063   spread of lake baselines
  residual   0.00125    0.035   spread of crayfish within a lake

Their ratio is the intraclass correlation, 0.76. Read it two ways. Three quarters of the variation in growth that is not explained by range is lake-to-lake. Equivalently, two crayfish from the same lake are 0.76 correlated, so they are nowhere near independent observations. That is the single number that tells you a plain one-way ANOVA on 84 crayfish would have been wrong.

3. WHAT THE RANDOM EFFECT DID TO THE FIXED EFFECT

This is the part students most need and the part that is easiest to get wrong. The random intercept (1|lake) did not change the range test at all. The additive fixed model range + lake and the mixed model give F = 27.6 on 1 and 80 df, p around one in a million, with the same contrast of 0.043 and the same SE of 0.008. So on these data the mixed model bought nothing for the hypothesis test. What it changed is the reporting of lake and the population you may generalise to.

The reason is that range varies within every lake. Every crayfish carries information about Native versus Invasive, so the model counts all of them: 84 observations minus one intercept minus three lake levels gives 80 df. The random intercept only shifts each lake up or down. It never touches the slope.

4. WHERE IT STOPS BEING USEFUL, AND WHY THAT MATTERS IN THIS EXAMPLE

A random intercept assumes the invasive advantage is the same in every lake. It fits parallel lines. The week 9 interaction said that is false: the advantage is +0.07 in Big and High and zero in Papoose, p = 0.000017. So the random-intercept model is quietly the additive model, and it is assuming away the most interesting finding in the data.

The fix is a random slope, (1|lake) + (1|lake:range), which lets each lake have its own range effect. Its variance component, SD 0.030, is the interaction expressed as a variance. Now the range test is against lake-to-lake disagreement about the advantage. The estimate stays 0.046 but the SE triples to 0.025, the df drop from 80 to 2, and p becomes 0.21. The honest reading is that the general claim "invasives grow faster in Wisconsin lakes" rests on three lakes, one of which disagrees, and three replicates cannot carry it.

5. SO IS THE RANDOM EFFECT USEFUL HERE?

As a demonstration, very. As the analysis to report, no. Three lakes is too few levels to estimate a variance well. The profile confidence interval on the lake SD runs from 0.027 to 0.152, a six-fold range, so the number you would be reporting is barely estimated. The fixed two-way model with its interaction is the right analysis for this study, because it answers the question the design can actually answer: what happened in these three lakes. The mixed model's contribution is to make you state that limit out loud.

6. WHERE IT IS USEFUL

The urchins in Parts 1 to 6 are the real case. Sixteen patches is enough levels to estimate a variance, the patch variance is itself a finding with ICC 0.50, the shrunken patch estimates are better than the raw means, and the design is one a two-factor ANOVA cannot express because patch is nested in treatment rather than crossed with it. The rule the lecture gives is the right one. Ask whether you care about these particular levels or about how much levels vary, and whether you have at least five or six of them. If the answer to the first is "these levels" or the answer to the second is "no", keep the factor fixed.

THE TWO SENTENCES TO CARRY INTO CLASS

A fixed effect gives you the levels; a random effect gives you a variance and changes who the result is about. A random intercept assumes the treatment effect is identical in every group, so if the groups disagree about the treatment you need a random slope, and then the groups, not the observations, are your replicates.

SAYING THE RANDOM EFFECT IN ENGLISH — CRAYFISH THAT WORKED, URCHIN PATCHES, DRUG DOSE

THE ONE IDEA

A fixed effect answers "which levels differ, and by how much". A random effect answers "how much do levels like these differ from one another". So the English for a random effect is never about a named level. It is always a sentence about spread, and it always has the same three parts:

  1. How big the spread is, in the units of the response (the SD, not the variance).
  2. How that compares with the spread inside a level (the residual SD, or the ICC).
  3. What that means for a level you did not sample.

If you cannot write part 3 sensibly, the factor should not be random. That test settles the drug-dose question below.


1. THE CRAYFISH, IF PAPOOSE HAD AGREED

This is a what-if, not the real data. I shifted the Papoose invasive crayfish up so that the invasive advantage in Papoose matched Big and High (about +0.071 mm/day in all three), left everything else alone, and refitted. The interaction disappears (F = 0.008, p = 0.99).

  lmer(growth_per_day ~ range + (1|lake) + (1|lake:range))

  lake SD             0.035 mm/day     (profile 95% CI 0.014 to 0.085)
  lake:range SD       0.000            (the lakes agree about the advantage)
  residual SD         0.031 mm/day
  ICC                 0.56
  Invasive - Native   0.071 mm/day, SE 0.0075, 95% CI 0.033 to 0.110, p = 0.018 on about 2 df

What you could then say about LAKE, in English:

  "Lakes differed in how fast crayfish grew. A typical lake sat about 0.035 mm/day above or below the average lake, which is slightly more than the difference between two crayfish in the same lake (0.031). A little over half of the variation in growth not explained by range was lake-to-lake."

  "In a lake we did not sample, we would expect average growth somewhere around 0.26 mm/day, give or take about 0.07 (two lake SDs), so roughly 0.19 to 0.33."

What you could say about the lake-by-range term:

  "The invasive advantage did not vary detectably among lakes (SD estimated at zero), so the advantage looks like a property of the crayfish and not of the lake."

And about RANGE, which is the payoff:

  "Invasive crayfish grew 0.071 mm/day faster than native crayfish, and because the advantage was consistent across lakes we expect it to hold in similar lakes we did not sample."

That last clause is what making lake random buys you. With lake fixed, the same data let you say only "in Big, High and Papoose". The lake effect itself is not the prize. It is the thing you had to measure in order to earn the generalisation.

What you still could NOT say, even though it worked:

  - "Papoose is slower than Big." There is no test of lake against lake and no Tukey. The model does give a predicted deviation for each lake (Big +0.017, High +0.023, Papoose -0.040) and you may plot those, but they are descriptions of your sample, not hypotheses tested.
  - "Lake has a significant effect, p = ...". The random-effect row has no F and no p. You report the SD and its confidence interval.
  - Anything precise about the lake SD. With three lakes the CI runs 0.014 to 0.085, a six-fold range. And the zero for lake:range is three lakes failing to disagree, which is weak evidence that lakes in general agree.

One caution about the output: because the lake:range variance came out at exactly zero, anova() with Satterthwaite collapses back to 80 df and reports p = 8e-16. Kenward-Roger keeps the honest 2 df and gives p = 0.018. Both say "significant"; only the second is counting lakes.

WITH FIVE LAKES

I then added two invented lakes (simulated, same advantage, same within-lake noise, baselines 0.21 and 0.29) to see what more lakes buy. Nothing about the English changes. The numbers behind it firm up:

                          3 lakes agreeing      5 lakes agreeing
  Invasive - Native       0.071                 0.068
  95% CI                  0.033 to 0.110        0.049 to 0.088
  df for range            about 2               about 4
  p                       0.018                 0.0007
  lake SD (95% CI)        0.035 (0.014-0.085)   0.037 (0.019-0.073)
  lake:range SD           0.000                 0.007

The estimate hardly moves. The confidence interval on the advantage halves and the interval on the lake SD tightens. More lakes do not change what you say; they change how much anyone should believe it. Lakes are the replicates for every sentence that begins "in lakes generally", so that is where the sample size has to go. Adding crayfish within the same three lakes would not help.


2. THE URCHIN PATCHES — THE REAL NUMBERS

  lmer(algae ~ treat + (1|patch))     16 patches, 4 per treatment, 5 quadrats per patch

  patch SD       17.2 percentage points of algal cover   (95% CI 8.9 to 22.8)
  residual SD    17.3 percentage points
  ICC            0.50
  treat          F = 2.72 on 3 and 12 df, p = 0.091
  means          0%: 39.2    33%: 19.0    66%: 21.6    100%: 1.3    (SE 9.4 each)

What you can say about PATCHES, in English:

  "Patches that received the same urchin treatment still differed from each other a great deal. A typical patch sat about 17 percentage points of algal cover above or below what its treatment predicts."

  "That is as large as the difference between two quadrats in the same patch (also 17 points). Half of the variation in algal cover that urchin density does not explain is patch-to-patch. The reef is patchy at the scale of whole patches, not only at the scale of quadrats."

  "Two quadrats from the same patch are correlated 0.50, so five quadrats in a patch are nowhere near five independent looks at a treatment. The replicates for the urchin treatment are the 16 patches, which is why the test has 12 df and not 76."

  "If we set up a new urchin-removal patch on this reef, we would not expect 39% cover. We would expect something centred on 39% but anywhere from roughly 5% to 75% (39 plus or minus two patch SDs)."

The fourth sentence is the one students miss. The treatment mean tells you about the average patch. The patch SD tells you how far any single patch can sit from that average. Look at the four removal patches: 34, 62, 2 and 58% cover. The model is describing exactly that scatter with one number.

And the patch SD explains the fixed-effect result. Means that run from 39% to 1% look like a huge effect, yet p = 0.09. The SE on each treatment mean is 9.4 points, and almost all of that comes from patch-to-patch variation divided among only four patches. So the English for the whole study is:

  "Algal cover fell from 39% with no urchins to 1% at full density, but patches treated alike varied so much (SD 17 points) that four patches per treatment could not separate the treatments with confidence. More patches, not more quadrats, is what the next experiment needs."

What you cannot say: "patch 15 differs from patch 14". Nobody would want to. Patch 15 has no meaning outside this study. That is the sign that patch really is random.


3. DRUG DOSE AS THE RANDOM EFFECT, WITH MALE VERSUS FEMALE

Suppose you fitted response ~ sex + (1|dose). Apply the three-part template.

  1. Spread: "Across the doses we used, mean response varied with an SD of X units."
  2. Comparison: "That is larger or smaller than the animal-to-animal SD within a dose."
  3. A new level: "At some other dose drawn from the same population of doses, we would expect the response to be within about 2X of the average."

Sentence 3 falls apart. There is no population of doses that yours were drawn from. You chose 0, 5, 10 and 20 mg on purpose. And look at what sentences 1 and 2 leave out:

  - Direction. The model does not know that more drug gives more response or less. A variance has no sign.
  - Order. It does not know 10 mg lies between 5 and 20. The doses are treated as interchangeable labels, like patches.
  - Shape. No slope per mg, no threshold, no plateau, no ED50.
  - Comparisons. No "20 mg differs from control".

So the reason you cannot get to a satisfying English sentence about drug concentration is that there isn't one. All the random effect can tell you is "dose matters by about this much", and every question a pharmacologist actually has is about which dose and in which direction. Those are fixed-effect questions. Dose should be fixed: a factor if you want to compare doses, or better a number (a slope, "response rises 1.2 units per mg") if you want the dose-response curve.

What the model would say about SEX: "Averaged over the doses, females responded Y units more than males." That part reads normally. But (1|dose) assumes the sex difference is identical at every dose, which is the parallel-lines assumption from the crayfish again. If you added (1|dose:sex) you would get "the sex difference changed from dose to dose, SD Z", and the result people care most about (females respond more at high doses but not at low ones) would be reduced to one number with no direction. With dose fixed, that same finding is the sex-by-dose interaction and you can describe it dose by dose.

The same problem as the crayfish also applies. Four or five doses is too few levels to estimate a variance.

What IS random in a drug study: the things you replicated without caring which ones you got. Animal (if each is measured more than once), cage, litter, batch, clinic, day. A sensible model is

  response ~ dose * sex + (1|cage)

and the English for cage is the same as for patch: "cages treated alike differed by SD so much, so cages and not animals are the replicates".

When a drug factor CAN be random: you screen 30 compounds picked from a chemical library and want to know how much compounds vary in potency. Then "compound" is a sample from a population, part 3 of the template makes sense ("a new compound from this library would typically be within..."), and (1|compound) is right. The difference is whether the levels are a sample of a population or a set of chosen settings.


4. THE TEST TO APPLY EVERY TIME

Imagine throwing away your levels and drawing a fresh set. Is it still the same study?

  Patches     yes. Sixteen other patches on the reef would do.          RANDOM
  Lakes       mostly yes, if you want "lakes in general", and           RANDOM in principle,
              you have enough of them.                                   fixed with only three
  Doses       no. 3, 7, 11 and 40 mg is a different experiment.          FIXED
  Sex         no. There is nothing else to draw.                         FIXED

And the sentence each kind of effect lets you write:

  fixed     "Level A differs from level B by this much."
  random    "Levels like these differ from one another by about this much, so a new one would fall in this range, and the fixed effects I report hold across such levels and not only in the ones I measured."

  The catch is that a block analysis assumes the treatment effect is the same in every block. The block is only supposed
  to shift everything up or down. For the real crayfish that assumption fails:
  
  - The real data: the advantage was +0.07 in Big and High and zero in Papoose. The block model range + (1|lake) assumes
    that disagreement away and reports F = 27.6 on 80 df.
  - The what-if where Papoose agrees: the block model would have been the right analysis. The lakes shift the baseline,
    the advantage is the same everywhere, and you report one invasive advantage.
  
  There is one real difference between the two designs, in what the block × treatment term means:
  
  - Willow: each stream has one reach per treatment, so stream:treatment is the reach. Reach-to-reach noise and "streams
    disagree about willow" are the same nine numbers, and you can't separate them. You have to assume there is no
    interaction.
  - Crayfish: there are many crayfish in each lake × range cell, so the interaction can be separated from

  So the crayfish design let you check the blocking assumption, and it failed. The willow design, with one reach per
  stream per treatment, can't check it.
  
  That also changes how you think about lake. A block is a nuisance you include to soak up noise and then ignore. In the
  crayfish study, whether the advantage holds from lake to lake turned out to be the finding, so lake behaved like a
  second factor of interest, not a block.
